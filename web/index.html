Pricing and hedging an up-and-out barrier call

An up-and-out call pays like an ordinary call, unless the stock touches the barrier before expiry, in which case it pays nothing. Change the contract below and watch the project's three findings: how often the barrier is checked changes the price, the Greeks explode near the barrier as expiry approaches, and delta hedging leaves fat tails on both sides.

1 Pricer

What to look for: switch between continuous and daily checks. A path can cross the barrier and come back between two checks, so the less often the barrier is checked, the more the option is worth. The Broadie–Glasserman–Kou shift reproduces the simulated price from the closed form.

2 Greeks explorer

Time to expiry

What to look for: with a year to go, delta turns negative well below the barrier, because a rally adds more knockout risk than value. Now shorten the time to expiry: just below the barrier the option is worth a lot, at the barrier it is worth nothing, and delta and gamma explode. Continuous barrier shown.

3 Hedging simulator

What to look for: sell each option at its model price and delta-hedge it with the stock. Rebalancing more often shrinks the vanilla call's hedging error like 1/√n, but helps the barrier option much less. Its P&L has fat tails on both sides, driven by paths that come close to the barrier. The barrier contract here is checked at each daily close. 2,000 simulated paths.

Method. Closed form for the continuously monitored up-and-out call: Haug (2007). Discrete monitoring via the Broadie–Glasserman–Kou (1997) barrier shift H·e±0.5826σ√Δt. Monte Carlo uses exact GBM steps, antithetic pairs (standard errors on pair averages) and, for the continuous barrier, the Brownian-bridge crossing probability. Greeks are central finite differences of the closed form. All random numbers are seeded, so results are reproducible.

The JavaScript here is a port of the project's Python package. Its tests check it against 1,098 values computed by that package. More detail in the notebook.
